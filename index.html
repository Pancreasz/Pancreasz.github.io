Project

Fly Brain

I've been getting interested in neuroscience, and Google and Janelia recently open-sourced a full map of the fruit fly brain (the MaleCNS connectome), so I wanted to play with it. This project taught me a lot about the brain. The fact that amazed me most is that, even with the whole wiring map, we still don't really know how the brain processes information. We can see the inputs and the outputs, but we don't actually know what's happening in between, and the fly brain is the same. So the approach is to map the brain's inputs and outputs, label which neurons do what, and then use them.

In this project I didn't use the whole brain, only the parts I needed: the escape circuit (looming-detector neurons → the giant fiber, which makes the fly jump away from the swatter), the smell circuit (odour receptor neurons → projection neurons, so the fly can sniff out the cheese), and the vision circuits (LC10 eye neurons → DNa02 to steer toward the cheese, and LPLC4 → DNp07 to land on it).

I just wanted to try out the fly brain, and then a crazy idea came to mind: what if we were the fly, and the fly tried to swat us instead of us swatting them? So I made a little game about it. I also visualized the neurons, so you can see when each one is active and what it does.

SNN-Pyramid

I started this project after I found out about neuromorphic chips. They work more like our brain does, computing fast while using far less energy than a normal chip. That really impressed me, and I think this is where the next generation of chips is going. So I looked into where the research is right now, and that's how I found spiking neural networks (SNNs).

What surprised me is how closely they copy the brain. Neurons don't pass numbers around, they fire spikes, just 1s and 0s over time, and the network counts the spikes to make its decision. Most neurons stay quiet most of the time, and that's where the power saving comes from. The idea is really simple, but it works.

I wanted to make this more efficient, and I landed on the encoder, the step that turns an image into spikes before it goes into the SNN. There are a few common ones. Rate coding makes bright pixels fire more often. Direct coding feeds the raw pixel values in at every step and lets the first layer do the spiking. Latency coding makes the strongest pixels fire first. DCT-SNN splits the image into frequencies and sends the low ones first.

Then I remembered the Gaussian pyramid from my image processing class. You blur the image and shrink it to half size, then do it again, so you get a stack of smaller, blurrier copies (28×28, 14×14, 7×7 here). Take the difference between each level and the next one and you're left with just the detail at that scale. I tried sending those layers into the SNN one after another, and it worked. On energy it still didn't clearly beat the best encoders out there, but I got the accuracy as close as I could.

You might notice something is off about the pyramid: it's upside down. That's one of my findings. The coarse layer doesn't help the classification much, so I flipped the order and the model gets the fine detail first. That helped, but it didn't fix the encoder on its own, so I went back to the papers and found methods that take the fine layer and keep adding the other layers on top of it. Right now I'm getting the network ready to run on an FPGA, so I can measure the energy for real instead of estimating it, and learn how SNNs and FPGAs fit together.

      Ear Biometric

      Tak hospital · newborns in the NICU

      This research project was developed in collaboration with a hospital in Tak province to solve a highly specific problem: identifying newborns in the NICU who are wearing oxygen masks. The hospital already utilized a face biometric system, but the masks obscured the infants' faces, rendering the current system unusable. They wanted to research and implement ear biometrics as an alternative, as the physical contours, ridges, and shapes of the ear are highly unique to each individual and remain visible. The project extended down to the hardware level; we integrated an Intel RealSense depth camera to calculate spatial distances and ensure the ear was captured at the precise angle required for a valid scan.

      The system pipeline starts with the camera feed, where we deploy a YOLOv8 object detection model to locate, segment, and crop the ear from the background. We then pass this cropped image to our embedding algorithm. The mathematical precision of this step works flawlessly, but it also creates a strict constraint: the ear must be aligned at the exact same angle during both the initial registration and any subsequent identification attempts. Once the embedding vector is generated, we store it in a Dockerized PostgreSQL vector database alongside the patient's records. For future identifications, the system captures a new scan, generates a fresh vector, and compares it against our database to find a match.

      I had the opportunity to travel to the hospital in Tak to test the system in the field. While the software and detection models executed perfectly, we encountered a highly unpredictable real-world variable: the babies wouldn't stay still. This constant movement made capturing a clean, perfectly aligned shot extremely difficult. Furthermore, during our first iteration, we discovered a flaw tied to ear color—when the babies cried, their ears would flush bright red, which shifted the pixel values enough to disrupt the matching process. To solve this, we updated our preprocessing pipeline to extract the Value (brightness) channel, essentially converting the image to grayscale, and applied CLAHE to normalize the shadows. This highlighted the physical ridges while completely stripping away any skin color or redness.

      For the application's architecture, we built the desktop UI using React and TSX wrapped in Electron, supported by a TypeScript backend. Because of the computational workload, we offloaded all camera communication, YOLO model inference, and image preprocessing to dedicated Python scripts. Rather than relying on a deep learning model for the biometric signature, we utilized a HOG (Histogram of Oriented Gradients) descriptor. This analyzes the edge orientations of the grayscale image to create the final, high-dimensional ear embedding.

      MoCA Test

      Montreal Cognitive Assessment

      This is the project that got me into research. My professor gave me the problem and guided me the whole way through.

      The problem is simple: how can we make the MoCA test more efficient? MoCA is a 30-point paper test doctors use to screen for early memory and thinking problems like dementia. The patient finishes it in about ten minutes, but then medical staff have to check every answer by hand, and that takes a lot of time. So what if an app did exactly that? It scores the patient, gives the result straight to the medical staff, and gets rid of the paper too, so we don't throw a test sheet away every time someone takes it.

      I built it as a Flutter app with a Python Flask server behind it. The hardest part is the Clock Drawing Test, where the patient draws a clock showing ten past eleven. Everyone draws differently: shaky circles, numbers squeezed to one side, hands the wrong length. A hand-written algorithm can't cover all of that, so we turned to deep learning instead. A DenseNet-201 model scored the clocks with about 90% accuracy, better than ResNet50, EfficientNet-B7 and XGBoost on the same test set, and the work became ASDL-2, the follow-up to my first paper, ASDL.

      Noise Cancellation on FPGA

      Signals & Systems mini-project · KMUTNB · 2024

      This was a mini-project for Introduction to Signals & Systems, done with two classmates. The plan was simple on paper. An INMP441 microphone sends audio over I2S into a Cyclone V FPGA, an FIR filter on the chip removes the noise, and the cleaned audio goes out through a CH343 USB-UART to a laptop.

      Getting the sound out turned out to be the hard part. Our 16-bit samples kept arriving scrambled, so we kept only the top 8 bits and put a FIFO in front of the UART. Our first board didn't have enough RAM for that FIFO, so we moved to a DE0-CV, then tried baud rate after baud rate until around 460k finally gave clean data.

      The filter is where we had to compromise. The VHDL version gave garbage until we switched the coefficients to fixed point, and even then it wasn't good enough. So the final filtering ran in Python on the recorded audio (a 101-tap band-pass from 300 to 2000 Hz). The FFT showed our 4 kHz test tone gone, with a little noise left over.

      Research & Publication

      ASDL-2

      P. Paeyai, T. Chansuk, P. Vorasayan, K. Srisomboon, W. Lee, V. Sa-ing. "Alzheimer Screening Using Clock Drawing Scores Based on Deep Learning: ASDL-2." 2025 International Conference on Information Technologies (InfoTech-2025).

      ASDL-2 extends our earlier ASDL work from binary screening to a structured 0–3 clinical scoring rubric for the clock drawing: clock shape, number placement and hand position. That reframes the task as four-class classification, with clinically interpretable output instead of a single AD/HC label.

      We benchmarked DenseNet-201 against ResNet50, EfficientNet-B7 and XGBoost on a held-out test set from the same hospital source, and DenseNet-201 led on every metric (F1 90.61 vs. 83.50, 78.83 and 47.50). The raw dataset had a 200:24 class imbalance between score-3 and score-0 drawings, so we rebalanced the training set with flipping, cropping and random-erasing augmentation before it reached the model. As in the first paper, we used Score-CAM to keep the finer-grained scoring model's decisions clinically interpretable.

      Ear Recognition

      P. Paeyai, K. Srisomboon, T. Chansuk, W. Lee, K. Wasayangkool, V. Sa-ing. "Ear Biometric Recognition Using Deep Learning." In press, 2026 14th International Electrical Engineering Congress (iEECON 2026).

      We benchmarked ResNet-50, EfficientNet-B0 and ViT-B/16 for ear biometric verification, telling genuine pairs from impostor pairs, on the AMI Ear Dataset (107 subjects, 7 images each). EfficientNet-B0 gave the best overall result (AUC 0.9893, accuracy 97.82%, F1 0.7986), ahead of ResNet-50 (AUC 0.9816). ViT-B/16 had the highest recall (0.8032) but the weakest precision (0.7398): more tolerant of variation, at the cost of more false accepts.

      This result is what selected EfficientNet as the backbone for the Ear Biometric system in my projects. The two are one research line, not two independent efforts.
